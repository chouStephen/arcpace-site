Short answer: dress for about 15–20°F warmer than it is, cover hands and ears, start into the wind, and carry a light for dark mornings. Watch for ice, numb or waxy-white skin (frostnip), and shivering with clumsiness or confusion (hypothermia). Wet plus wind is the dangerous combination.

Cold-weather running is mostly comfortable once you're moving: you make plenty of heat. The problems come from the edges: the first mile before you warm up, the last mile when you're wet and slowing down, and the parts of you that don't make much heat at all, like fingers, ears and face.

Dress for mile two

You'll feel 15 to 20°F warmer within the first mile, so if you're comfortable standing at the door, you're overdressed. Aim to feel slightly cold when you start.

  • Layers that move sweat: a synthetic or wool base layer against the skin, never cotton, which stays wet and cold.
  • A windproof outer layer matters more than a thicker one. Wind strips heat faster than cold air alone.
  • Hands, ears and neck first: gloves or mittens (mittens are warmer), a headband or beanie over the ears, and a buff.
  • Tights below about 40°F for most runners; shorts above. Legs warm up fast.

What to wear at each temperature.

Wind: start into it

On an out-and-back, run into the wind first while you're dry, and come home with it behind you once you've sweated. A headwind on the way home, when your shirt is wet, is how a fine run turns into a cold one.

Frostbite and frostnip

  • Frostnip comes first: skin goes numb, red then pale. Warm it and it recovers.
  • Frostbite is skin that freezes: numb, white or grayish-yellow, waxy or hard. Fingers, toes, ears, nose and cheeks go first. Get indoors and rewarm gently in warm (not hot) water. Don't rub it, and get medical care.
  • Frostbite needs the air to be below freezing; wind chill speeds it up. On the National Weather Service chart, exposed skin can freeze in about 30 minutes at a wind chill near -20°F, and within minutes at much lower wind chills.

Hypothermia

Hypothermia is your core getting too cold. It can happen well above freezing when you're wet, it's windy, and you've slowed down or stopped, such as at the end of a long run in cold rain.

  • Early: strong shivering, numb hands, fumbling.
  • Getting serious: clumsiness, slurred speech, confusion, unusual sleepiness, and shivering that stops. That's an emergency: call 911.
  • Prevent it: carry a light shell on long or wet runs, plan loops near home, and change out of wet clothes right after.

Ice and darkness

  • Bridges, shaded corners and dawn pavement freeze first. Black ice looks like wet road.
  • On slick ground, shorten your stride and land under your hips. Traction devices that slip over shoes help on packed snow and ice.
  • Winter runs are often in the dark: wear reflective gear and a light so drivers see you, and a headlamp so you see the ice.

Breathing cold air

Cold, dry air can make your chest burn or tighten. A buff over the mouth warms and moistens the air. If you wheeze or cough after cold runs, some people's airways narrow with exercise in the cold, and it's worth seeing a doctor.

Warm up inside

A few minutes of easy movement indoors before you go, such as leg swings, lunges and skipping, means you start warm and get through the cold first mile faster.
