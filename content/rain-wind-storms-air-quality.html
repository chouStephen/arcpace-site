Short answer: run in the rain with a cap, good socks and anti-chafe, but watch cold rain. In wind, start into it and run by effort. If you can hear thunder, get inside and wait 30 minutes after the last rumble. When the Air Quality Index is above 100, go easier; above 150, cut back; above 200, run indoors.

Rain

Rain itself barely slows you; it's the extras that make a wet run bad.

  • A cap with a brim keeps rain out of your eyes better than anything else.
  • Synthetic or wool socks, not cotton, and shoes you don't mind soaking. Wet feet blister more.
  • Anti-chafe on feet, thighs, underarms and anywhere a strap or seam rubs. Wet fabric chafes much faster.
  • Be seen: rain cuts drivers' visibility. Wear something bright or reflective.
  • Cold rain is the risky kind. At 40 to 50°F, wet and windy, you lose heat fast once you slow down. A light shell and a short loop near home keep a cold rain run from turning into hypothermia.
  • Footing: painted road lines, metal grates, wet leaves and wooden bridges are slippery.

Wind

  • A headwind costs more than a tailwind gives back. A windy out-and-back is slower overall than a calm one, so run the windward half by effort and let the pace go.
  • In the cold, start into the wind while you're dry, and come home with it behind you once you're sweaty.
  • Gusts are what knock you off balance and tire you out. Strong, gusty days are good days for sheltered routes.
  • Running in a group? Taking turns at the front into a headwind saves a real amount of effort.

Thunderstorms

Lightning is the one weather danger that has no gear fix.

  • If you can hear thunder, you can be struck. Lightning can strike miles from the storm, before the rain arrives and after it passes.
  • Get inside a building, or a car with a metal roof. Trees, picnic shelters, open fields and hilltops are not safe.
  • Wait 30 minutes after the last thunder before heading back out.
  • Check before you go. Summer afternoons are the usual time for storms; an early-morning run often avoids them.

ArcPace caps the run score for any hour with thunderstorms, whatever the temperature, so a storm hour never looks like a good time to run.

Smoke and air quality

Wildfire smoke and summer ozone don't show up on a thermometer, and when you run you breathe far more air than at rest, so you take in more of what's in it.

Air Quality Index (AQI) categories from AirNow (U.S. EPA), with a running guide.

  • Ozone builds up through sunny afternoons, so smoggy summer days are usually better in the early morning.
  • Smoke can be bad at any hour and can be worse indoors than you'd expect without filtering.
  • If you notice coughing, a tight chest or unusual shortness of breath, stop.
