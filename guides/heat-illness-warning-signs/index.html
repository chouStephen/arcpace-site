Heat illness warning signs for runners: when to stop

Updated 2026-10-04 · General guidance, not medical advice

Short answer: stop running at the first signs of heat exhaustion: heavy sweating, weakness, dizziness, nausea, a headache, or chills and goosebumps in the heat. Get into shade, cool down and sip water. Confusion, slurred speech or collapse is heat stroke, a medical emergency. Call 911 and start cooling the person immediately.

Heat illness runs from uncomfortable to life-threatening, and it can move quickly. The point of knowing the signs is to stop early, when stopping is enough. This is general safety information, not medical advice: when in doubt, get help.

Heat cramps

What you notice: painful spasms, usually in the calves, thighs or stomach, with heavy sweating.

What to do: stop, get out of the sun, stretch gently, and drink water or a sports drink. Don't go back to a hard effort that day. Cramps can be the first warning that heat exhaustion is coming.

Heat exhaustion

What you notice: heavy sweating; weakness or unusual tiredness; cool, pale, clammy skin; a fast, weak pulse; muscle cramps; dizziness; nausea or vomiting; headache; fainting. Many runners also describe goosebumps or chills while hot, which is a sign to stop.

What to do:

  • Stop running and get somewhere cooler: shade, or air conditioning if you can.
  • Loosen or remove extra clothing.
  • Cool your skin with cold, wet cloths, a cold shower or a cool bath.
  • Sip water or a sports drink.
  • Get medical help right away if you vomit, if it gets worse, or if it lasts longer than an hour.

Heat stroke

What you notice, usually in someone else: confusion, slurred speech, strange behavior, stumbling, collapse or seizures; a throbbing headache; a body temperature above about 103°F; hot, red skin that can be dry or still sweaty; a rapid, strong pulse. During exercise, people with heat stroke are often still sweating, so don't wait for dry skin.

What to do:

  1. Call 911. Heat stroke can kill, and delay makes it worse.
  2. Cool them, fast, starting now. The best method is immersing the body in cold or ice water up to the neck. If that's impossible, get them into shade, soak them with cold water, and put ice or cold, wet towels on the neck, armpits and groin, replacing them as they warm.
  3. Don't give fluids to someone who is confused or unconscious.
  4. Fanning helps evaporation in moderate heat, but in extreme heat (heat index in the high 90s or above) blowing hot air on someone can heat them further.

At races, medical teams treat heat stroke by cooling on site before transport, because how fast the body temperature comes down matters most.

Too much water: hyponatremia

The opposite problem looks similar. Drinking far more than you sweat, usually plain water over a long, slow race, can dilute the sodium in your blood. Signs include a worsening headache, nausea, bloating or swelling in the hands and feet, confusion and seizures. It's an emergency, and more water makes it worse. Drink to thirst, and take salt on runs over about an hour.

Who is at higher risk

  • Runners who aren't used to the heat yet: the first hot weeks of the year, or a trip somewhere hotter.
  • Anyone running hard: races and fast workouts make far more heat than easy runs.
  • Anyone recently ill, short on sleep, or after alcohol.
  • Some medications, including some for blood pressure, allergies and mood. Ask your doctor or pharmacist if you're unsure.

Preventing it

Most heat illness on a run comes from going too hard for the conditions. Slow down by effort, run when it's cooler, wet your skin, and give yourself time to adapt. How to run in the heat covers the details, and this table shows how much to slow down.

Questions

What are the first signs of heat exhaustion while running?

Heavy sweating, weakness or unusual tiredness, dizziness, nausea, a headache, and cool, pale, clammy skin. Some runners get goosebumps or chills in the heat. Stop, get into shade and cool down.

How do you tell heat exhaustion from heat stroke?

Confusion. Heat exhaustion makes you weak and dizzy but you stay clear-headed. Heat stroke affects the brain: confusion, slurred speech, odd behavior, collapse or seizures, with a body temperature above about 103°F. The skin can still be sweaty in heat stroke during exercise.

What should you do for heat stroke?

Call 911, then cool the person as fast as you can: cold water or ice water immersion if there is any way to do it, otherwise soak them with cold water and put ice or cold wet towels on the neck, armpits and groin, in shade. Don't give fluids to someone who is confused or unconscious.

Can you drink too much water on a run?

Yes. Drinking far more than you sweat over a long run, especially plain water, can dilute the sodium in your blood (hyponatremia). Signs include a worsening headache, nausea, swelling, confusion and seizures. It's an emergency, and more water makes it worse.
