How to run in the heat: 12 ways to stay cool

Updated 2026-10-04 · General guidance, not medical advice

Short answer: run when the sun is low, slow down by effort rather than holding your normal pace, wet your skin and head, drink to thirst with some salt on longer runs, and give your body about two weeks of heat to adapt. Stop at the first sign of dizziness, confusion or chills.

Running in the heat is hard because your body has two jobs at once: moving you, and getting rid of the heat that moving makes. Blood that would go to your muscles goes to your skin instead, your heart rate climbs, and every mile costs more. You can't change the weather, but you can change when, how fast and how cool you run.

Before the run

  1. Pick the time, not just the day. The air is coolest around dawn, and the sun is low until mid-morning. Humidity is often highest at dawn, so check the dew point too: on some days a dry evening beats a muggy morning. A quick rule: add the temperature and dew point in °F. Below about 120 is ordinary; at 140 a 10:00/mile runner loses about 24 seconds a mile; at 160, about 55. The full table.
  2. Find shade. Direct sun adds real heat on top of the air temperature. A tree-lined trail at 80°F is a different run from open asphalt at 80°F. In ArcPace's model, 75°F with a 70°F dew point costs about 32 seconds a mile in shade and 36 in full sun.
  3. Start cool. A cold drink or a slushy before you go, a few minutes in air conditioning, or a cold, wet towel on your neck all lower your starting temperature. It buys you the first part of the run.
  4. Dress light. Light colors, loose, thin technical fabric, a cap or visor, sunglasses and sunscreen. Run shirtless or in a singlet if you're comfortable.

During the run

  1. Run by effort, not by pace. The pace you hold at 55°F is not an easy pace at 80°F with a 70°F dew point. Keep easy runs at an easy effort or heart rate and let the pace come back. Slower runners lose more seconds per mile in heat, not fewer, because they're out in it longer.
  2. Wet your skin. Sweat cools you only when it evaporates. Pouring water over your head, neck and arms helps the same way. A wet buff around the neck, or ice in your cap, works well.
  3. Drink to thirst. Carry water on runs over about 45 minutes in heat. Sip when you're thirsty instead of forcing a schedule.
  4. Take salt on long runs. Over about an hour, a sports drink, salt tabs or salty food helps replace what you sweat out. Drinking lots of plain water with no salt over many hours can dilute the sodium in your blood, which is dangerous.
  5. Plan loops. Loops past a car, house, fountain or store mean water and an easy way to stop. An out-and-back into the sun with no shade is the hardest kind of hot run.

Over the summer

  1. Give it two weeks. After about 10 to 14 days of running in the heat, with most of the change in the first week, you sweat sooner and more, your heart rate at a given pace drops, and the same run feels easier. Keep runs easy and shorter at first.
  2. Count recovery. Hot runs take more out of you. Sleep, food and an easier next day matter more in July than in October.
  3. Know your own risk. A recent illness, poor sleep, alcohol the night before, some medications, and being new to running all raise the risk of heat illness. On those days, go shorter, slower and earlier, or run indoors.

When to stop

Stop, get into shade and cool down if you feel dizzy, sick to your stomach, unusually weak, get goosebumps or chills in the heat, or stop making sense to yourself. Confusion, slurred speech or collapse in the heat is a medical emergency: call 911 and start cooling the person right away. The warning signs, in detail.

Race day in the heat

Most races fly a heat flag based on WBGT (wet bulb globe temperature): Green, Yellow, Red and Black. At Yellow, slow your goal; at Red, run for the finish, not a time; at Black, races are usually cancelled. What each flag means.

Questions

How long does it take to get used to running in the heat?

About 10 to 14 days of running in the heat, with most of the change in the first week. Your body starts sweating sooner and more, your heart rate at a given pace drops, and runs feel easier. Keep the runs easy and short at first.

Should I run in the morning or evening in summer?

Usually the early morning: the air is coolest and the sun is low. Humidity is often higher at dawn, though, and some evenings are drier. Compare temperature plus dew point for both, and remember that sun on the road adds heat too.

How much water should I drink on a hot run?

Drink to thirst rather than to a schedule. For runs over about an hour in the heat, carry water and take some salt. Drinking far more than you sweat can dangerously dilute the sodium in your blood.

Is it safe to run when it's 90 degrees?

It can be for a fit, heat-adapted runner on a short, slow run in shade, but the risk rises fast with humidity and sun. Run very early, go short, slow down a lot, and stop at the first sign of dizziness, confusion or chills.
